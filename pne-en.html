Pain neuroscience education.
A method with a dose.
And a clear limit.

PNE changes the concept of pain: from a marker of damage to a signal of protection. Alone, it moves pain very little. Added to exercise, meta-analyses show about 1 point on a 0 to 10 scale in the short term, with low to moderate certainty. 01 / WHAT IT IS

An education that changes the concept of pain.

Pain neuroscience education (PNE) is an educational intervention. It aims to change the concept of pain: from a marker of damage to a signal of the perceived need to protect tissue.

Definition

Moseley and Butler (2015) call it Explaining Pain. It uses conceptual-change strategies. The authors are clear that it is not behavioural or cognitive advice, and that it does not deny nociception. In that paper it increases knowledge, reduces catastrophising, and comes with short-term reductions in pain and disability.

The literature uses equivalent names: pain neuroscience education (PNE), therapeutic neuroscience education, pain science education (PSE), pain neurophysiology education and Explaining Pain. Reviews use them interchangeably. Bonatesta et al. (2022) and Núñez-Cortés et al. (2024) say PSE.

Origin

The first reference randomised trial is Moseley et al. (2004, The Clinical Journal of Pain), in chronic low back pain. Education changed attitudes (SOPA(R)), catastrophising (PCS), straight leg raise and forward flexion. The change in the RMDQ was small. The authors concluded that education alone was insufficient. The abstract gives no n, no sessions, no minutes and no number for the RMDQ.

The same year, Moseley (2004, European Journal of Pain) analysed 121 patients. Cognitive change correlated with physical change: r = 0.88 with straight leg raise and r = 0.79 with forward flexion.

The founding reviews are Louw et al. (2011), 8 studies and n = 401, a narrative synthesis, and Louw et al. (2016), 13 RCTs. In the sources used here, those two reviews do not provide a pooled effect this page can cite.

Where it sits

Model

Biopsychosocial

PNE is an educational tool inside the biopsychosocial model. It is not the whole model.

Not PNE

Cognitive functional therapy

CFT (O'Sullivan et al., 2018) is an integrated behavioural approach: making sense of pain, exposure with control, and lifestyle change. It includes a reconceptualisation close to PNE and joins it to exposure and behaviour change. That integration is what the data on PNE alone leave open.

The RESTORE trial (Kent et al., 2023, Lancet, n = 492) tested CFT, not stand-alone PNE. At 13 weeks the RMDQ was MD -4.6 (CI -5.9 to -3.4) with CFT and MD -4.6 (CI -5.8 to -3.3) with CFT plus biofeedback. The effect held at 52 weeks. The social saving was about AU$5,276 per person. Thiveos et al. (2024), 7 RCTs, report low to moderate certainty for pain and disability and high certainty for self-efficacy. The source does not give the effect size of that meta-analysis.

Louw et al. (2021, JOSPT) place PNE as an adjunct to exercise and movement for the patient, and as a central focus of clinician training. Nijs et al. (2020) propose integrating it with motivational interviewing. That paper is a practical guide, with no efficacy data, and the authors ask for trials.

02 / HOW IT WORKS

Threat falls. The quiz does not carry the outcome.

The theory is a chain: perceived threat falls, catastrophising and fear of movement fall, activity and exposure rise, and pain and disability fall with them. Mediation analyses test that chain.

RESOLVE: what mediated

Cashin et al. (2023) mediated the RESOLVE trial (Bagg et al., 2022): 276 adults with chronic low back pain, education plus graded sensorimotor retraining versus sham. Four of 7 variables mediated pain and 5 of 7 mediated disability. Together they explained most of the effect. The abstract does not name the ones that dropped out.

In RESOLVE itself, pain at 18 weeks was -1.0 (CI -1.5 to -0.4) versus sham, with 12 weekly sessions (Bagg et al., 2022). Venter et al. (2026) found no clear effect modifiers. Self-perception of the back was a possible modifier (β 0.18).

PREVENT: acute pain

Cashin et al. (2022) mediated PREVENT (Traeger et al., 2019, JAMA Neurology, n = 202): 2 hours of education versus placebo education in high-risk acute low back pain. Catastrophising mediated, -0.64 (CI -1.31 to -0.15), as did back beliefs, -0.51. Self-efficacy did not. To gain 2 RMDQ points, the MCID in that analysis, it would take 8 PSEQ points, 11 BBQ points or 21 PCS points. Those are large changes.

The trial itself found pain at 3 months of -0.3 (CI -1.0 to 0.3). Disability was -1.6 at one week (CI -3.1 to -0.1) and -1.7 at 3 months (CI -3.2 to -0.2). There were no differences at 6 or 12 months.

The quiz is not the mechanism. Ram et al. (2023) pooled 14 studies and about 1,500 people. In the short term, change in pain neurophysiology knowledge was not associated with outcomes: pain r = -0.01 (CI -0.14 to 0.13), very low certainty; kinesiophobia r = 0.02; catastrophising r = -0.03, low certainty. What seems to count is changing beliefs about consequences and threat, and carrying that into behaviour.

Other paths

Murillo et al. (2023), in PNE plus exercise, found that kinesiophobia and distress linked to central sensitisation mediated disability and medication use. Catastrophising and pain intensity did not. The source does not give the size of those indirect effects.

Alalawi (2026) reanalysed the Indian RCT of Sidiq et al. (2024). In 46 participants who received education plus physiotherapy, emotional wellbeing (WHO-5) had a mediated causal effect of -1.66 (CI -2.8 to -0.72), about a quarter of the effect on disability. Self-efficacy did not mediate. No mediator explained pain. The recovery trajectories from the same material are in the Saudi section: the author is Saudi and the data are from India.

Núñez-Cortés et al. (2024, Neuroscience & Biobehavioral Reviews) analysed 11 studies. The number of target concepts was associated with change in pain (F = 4.45; p = 0.04; R² 26.07%). Only two concepts had their own effect on pain: transferring knowledge into an adaptive behaviour change, and pain reconceptualisation. No concept had an effect on disability.

Di-Bonaventura et al. (2025) compared 3 hours of PNE with a booklet in 66 participants. Plasma BDNF did not change (p = 0.708). Both groups improved on pain: PNE d = 0.70 and booklet d = 0.39.

What people say after receiving it

  • Rizzo et al. (2024), n = 20, after an education-based intervention: the themes included "are you suggesting my pain is not real?", "my pain is different", and doubt about how to carry it into daily life.
  • Ciolan et al. (2025), 9 studies and 188 participants, moderate confidence: effective communication, emotional support and empowerment.
  • Kühn et al. (2026), 16 studies and 329 participants, 6 synthesised findings. The source used here does not list those six findings.
  • Watson et al. (2019), in the metasynthesis: letting the patient tell their story helps reconceptualisation.
A neighbouring field, with large effects. Pain Reprocessing Therapy (Ashar et al., 2022, n = 151) goes beyond classical PNE. Versus placebo, g -1.14. Versus usual care, g -1.74. Pain-free or nearly pain-free: 66% with PRT, 20% with placebo and 10% with usual care. At one year: g -0.70 and -1.05. This is not pure PNE. It shows where the field is heading: reconceptualisation with exposure and active reappraisal.

Reading. Passing the knowledge quiz is not the mechanism. The chain that does appear in the mediation analyses runs through threat beliefs and behaviour.

03 / HOW IT IS DONE

Content, format and minutes.

Doing PNE means choosing a format, a content and a dose. The reviews do not agree on a single moderator, and the figures below stay with the outcome each review measured.

Formats

Group

In Romm et al. (2021) the group format moderated the effect on kinesiophobia (d = 0.80; CI 0.09 to 1.50). In that review, dose did not moderate the effect. In Ma et al. (2024) the group subgroup had a pain MD of -1.76. Salazar-Méndez et al. (2024, PeerJ) is only a protocol comparing group and individual delivery (n = 69). There are no results yet.

Online

de Oliveira Lima et al. (2021), 6 trials and n = 1,664, studied self-guided web education. Pain: SMD -0.23 in the short term and -0.26 in the medium term. Disability: -0.36. No added benefit over usual care. García-Lucas et al. (2026) tested 12 audiovisual lessons for pregnancy-related lumbopelvic pain (n = 211): pain MD -0.5 (CI -1.0 to 0.0) and kinesiophobia MD -1.4 (CI -2.3 to -0.4).

Virtual reality

McConnell et al. (2024) ran a feasibility study: 52 people enrolled, 32 completed, with no between-group differences. The full-text extraction puts the mean session at about 21 minutes. The number of sessions varied by therapist, so total minutes cannot be calculated.

Single session

Tomás-Rodríguez et al. (2024), n = 121, added 60 minutes of PNE to a back school. Pain did not fall. The CSI and the PCS did improve at 12 weeks.

Metaphors

Louw et al. (2019) surveyed clinicians: 19 of 27 responded (70%). Six of 11 metaphors had a mean above 3, and none stood out clearly. There is no winning metaphor. It is chosen with the person.

Doses that exceed the MCID

Núñez-Cortés et al. (2024, Pain) pooled 26 RCTs and n = 1,852, with PNE added to exercise for chronic spinal pain. The effect of exercise alone was small. The source does not give the figure for that small effect. Salazar-Méndez et al. (2023) pooled 23 studies and n = 2,352. Doses ranged from 40 to 720 minutes and the dose-effect relationship was linear. Each card is a different outcome. They are not added together.

Content

  • Reconceptualise: pain as protection, not as damage (Moseley and Butler, 2015).
  • Close each block with a translation into behaviour: what to do differently this week. That concept, with reconceptualisation, was one of the two with its own effect on pain (Núñez-Cortés et al., 2024).
  • Work on beliefs about the consequences of back pain, the largest mediator in RESOLVE (Cashin et al., 2023).
  • Avoid "injury", "degeneration" and "wear" (KNGF 2021). Say explicitly that the pain is real (Rizzo et al., 2024). Let the person tell their story (Watson et al., 2019).

What to measure

The rNPQ shows whether the content was understood. Ram et al. (2023) show that this knowledge change does not predict short-term pain, so it is not the clinical target.

04 / HOW IT IS APPLIED

Always with exercise or with active work.

In clinic, PNE is used with exercise or with other active work. JOSPT 2021 summarises that none of the studies of PNE alone improved pain, and that 5 of 6 studies that combined it with exercise or manual therapy did reduce pain.

A practical proposal, not a validated programme. The eight steps come from the sources cited on each line. Nobody has tested this package, as written here, as a single intervention.
  1. Who. Persistent pain for more than 3 months, especially with high kinesiophobia or catastrophising, or with a high risk of chronicity.This is how the 2026 Saudi guideline and KNGF profile 3 set the priority.
  2. Never alone. Always joined to exercise or to active work.JOSPT, grade A. No stand-alone PNE improved pain in that guideline's summary.
  3. Dose. Aim for about 150 minutes in total for disability and about 200 minutes for pain.Núñez-Cortés et al., 2024, Pain. Sessions of 60 minutes or longer when possible (Ma et al., 2024). Group format is an option and saves clinician time (Romm et al., 2021; Ma et al., 2024).
  4. Content. Reconceptualise pain as protection and close each block with a behaviour for that week.Núñez-Cortés et al., 2024. Beliefs about the consequences of back pain: Cashin et al., 2023.
  5. Language. Avoid injury, degeneration and wear. Validate that the pain is real. Let the person tell their story.KNGF 2021. Rizzo et al., 2024. Watson et al., 2019.
  6. Metaphors. Choose them with the person. None is superior.Louw et al., 2019. Cultural adaptation into Arabic is in Najem et al., 2025, and Basalem et al., 2025, in the Saudi section.
  7. Measure at the start and at 6 to 12 weeks: NRS, a region-specific disability scale, TSK-11 and PCS. The rNPQ stays as a comprehension check.Arabic versions of the TSK, PCS and CSI are in the table in the previous section. Ram et al., 2023.
  8. Expectation. A larger change in kinesiophobia than in pain is what the trials show. A minority respond.Watson et al. (2021) estimate that the proportion of people who respond runs from 18% to 45%. There was not enough evidence of true between-person differences in response.

For

Meta-analyses of PNE added to exercise give about 1 point on a 0 to 10 pain scale and moderate to large effects on kinesiophobia. In the dose reviews, the number of minutes has a coherent relationship with the effect.

Against

Cuenca-Martínez et al. (2023), an umbrella review of 16 reviews: stand-alone PNE is not significant and the reviews are of critically low quality. Riley et al. (2026): only 5 high-integrity RCTs, and no reliable evidence that PNE adds to a specific physical intervention on pain or function. Ram et al. (2023): knowledge is not associated with outcomes. When the comparator is another active education, the effect on pain and function drops away and kinesiophobia is what often remains.

Reading. PNE works as content inside an active intervention. A specific advantage of the neuroscience content over a good education that is not neuroscience is not shown for pain or function.

Limits that remain open

  • There are few comparisons against a credible active education with 12 month follow-up.
  • Long-term effects on pain and function are inconsistent. Wood and Hendrick (2019) find no long-term effect.
  • It is unknown which component is doing the work: content, alliance, exposure or dose.
  • Evidence in athletes is very thin. The volleyball trial in the New section is small and retrospectively registered.
  • There are no data on sex and the hormonal cycle in the response to PNE. Holmes et al. (2026) set this as an agenda, in an editorial, not in a trial.
  • Review quality is low (Cuenca-Martínez et al., 2023) and RCT integrity is doubtful (Riley et al., 2026).

Sport

Annex

The 2026 Saudi guideline

Aldera et al. (2026) answer question 3.11 on chronic non-specific low back pain. The guideline suggests using PNE and prioritising it for people at higher risk of disability and chronicity. The recommendation is conditional for, with low certainty. The evidence base is Wood and Hendrick (2019). The update search found no new studies. No adverse events were reported.

For the Saudi context, the guideline judged PNE probably not acceptable because it might increase the clinician's workload, although it cites a feasibility trial (GLITtER) that supported acceptability. It judged PNE feasible with training. Telehealth might improve access. There is a concern about equity in rural areas. Knowledge and practice

rNPQ

Alhowimel et al. (2021): 111 Saudi physiotherapists, mean 6.7 ± 2.2 out of 12. 90% scored 9 or less.

Practice

Alshehri et al. (2020), n = 304. Patient education is used by 82.0%, electrotherapy by 61.9%, and spinal mobilisation or manipulation by 57.8%. Biomedical and biopsychosocial orientations were relatively low. Clinicians with a stronger biopsychosocial orientation use CFT more. The source does not give the score for those orientations.

Patients

Asiri et al. (2026), n = 298. Each 10 points of TSK were associated with +1.17 points of pain. Kinesiophobia explained 11% to 12% of the variance in pain.

The Arabic-speaking world, which is not Saudi Arabia

Muhsen and Sheeha (2026) randomised 120 physiotherapy students in Jordan, not in Saudi Arabia. One 70 minute class gave an NNT of 2.7 for knowledge and 3.6 for attitudes. At 6 months only knowledge was maintained. The abstract extraction adds the adjusted knowledge difference: 2.46 (CI 1.68 to 3.24) after the class and 1.20 (CI 0.25 to 2.15) at 6 months. Muhsen et al. (2025) describe 216 Jordanian students.

Najem et al. (2025) is an F1000 preprint on culturally adapted Arabic PNE in Lebanon, with 12 patients and 8 physiotherapists. Basalem et al. (2025), a scoping review of 38 studies: educational interventions are the most culturally adapted (9 studies, 33%) and only 6 (22%) used adaptation frameworks.

No Arabic validation of the rNPQ or the COPI-Adult turned up. Without them, the knowledge mechanism cannot be measured in Arabic-speaking patients. The TSK-AV-11, the Arabic PCS and the Arabic CSI do have data, in the table in Dose.

A study proposal, not evidence. A design to discuss: first, Arabic validation of the rNPQ and the COPI-Adult. Then a feasibility pilot RCT in Riyadh, randomised and with a blinded assessor, in adults with chronic low back pain at medium or high risk: adapted PNE (about 200 minutes in total, in a group, in Arabic) plus exercise, versus the same contact time of biomedical and ergonomic education plus the same exercise. Feasibility outcomes would be recruitment, adherence, retention and acceptability. Exploratory clinical outcomes would be the Arabic RMDQ, NRS, TSK-AV-11, Arabic PCS, Arabic CSI, rNPQ, COPI and follow-up at 3, 6 and 12 months, with a preplanned mediation in the style of Cashin et al. A feasibility pilot does not need a power calculation. No sample size is proposed here. An athlete arm remains an option, with no figures.
